\section{When Should a Small Model Be the Default in an Agent?}
\label{sec:agentic}

The preceding sections show that \glspl{slm} can be compressed to run on commodity and edge hardware at a characterized cost (Sections~\ref{sec:compression}--\ref{sec:deployment}), and that the general benchmark record can establish neither their relative capability nor their fitness for agents (Section~\ref{sec:reported}). This section assesses hypothesis~H (Section~\ref{subsec:intro_thesis}) on agent-specific evidence. The premises and the conversion pipeline are those of \citet{belcak2025slm_agentic}, and the \gls{slm}-default, \gls{llm}-fallback design with cost per successful task as its metric is shared with \citet{sharma2025slm_agentic}; what this section adds is the grading of each line of evidence, including contrary results, the break-even and checking conditions of H, and the counterexamples of Section~\ref{subsec:agentic_counterexamples}.

\subsection{What Agents Delegate}
\label{subsec:agentic_delegation}

An agent is a control loop in which language models operate alongside tools, memory, and a scaffold of prompts and parsers. The model is mostly asked to perform bounded sub-tasks: choose a tool and its arguments, extract fields, classify an intent, rewrite a query, summarize a passage, produce a short patch, or check a step. \citet{belcak2025slm_agentic} characterize such calls as narrow, repetitive, and format-constrained, and estimate, by inspecting three open-source agents, that about 60\% of the \gls{llm} queries in MetaGPT, 40\% in Open Operator, and 70\% in Cradle could be handled by appropriately specialized \glspl{slm}. These are the authors' judgments of replaceability, not measurements and not an application of H1--H4, so we grade them \evD{}. We found no trace study measuring the distribution of call types in deployed agents by count, cost, or contribution to task success.

\subsection{The Premises}
\label{subsec:agentic_case}

\subsubsection{P1: Capability Sufficiency}
\label{subsubsec:agentic_capability}

The first premise is that a specialized \gls{slm} is good enough for the individual bounded call, not for the completed trajectory. Section~\ref{subsubsec:agentic_ev_fc} sets out the evidence, which is favorable for tool selection and argument filling but mostly developer-reported. Two further results bear on it. Controllable reasoning extends what can be delegated: Qwen3 \citep{qwen3_2025} exposes a thinking budget, and distilled reasoning models such as Phi-4-mini-reasoning \citep{xu2025phi4mini_reasoning} report competition-mathematics results at 3--8B that earlier models of that size did not reach (\evC). And specialization, not size alone, appears to carry the gain: a controlled study of Qwen3 models from 0.6B to 32B, published after our snapshot, finds supervised fine-tuning with \gls{lora} the strongest in-distribution adaptation for tool calling across the whole range \citep{laskar2026sftrl} (\evB). All of the per-call evidence concerns models below 10B or a single family.

\subsubsection{P2: Economics}
\label{subsubsec:agentic_economics}

\citet{belcak2025slm_agentic} estimate that serving a 7B \gls{slm} is 10--30$\times$ cheaper in latency, energy, and FLOPs than serving a 70--175B \gls{llm}, and \citet{sharma2025slm_agentic} estimate that cost per successful task falls by a similar factor; both are estimates rather than measurements (\evD). The break-even condition H2 (Equation~\ref{eq:breakeven}, Figure~\ref{fig:breakeven}) is how we state the premise instead, because four things that a per-token ratio omits decide the outcome. First, the unit is cost per \emph{completed task}: a small model that needs $a$ attempts per call, a check costing $c_V$, and escalation of a fraction $e$ of calls to the large model saves only $c_L - (a\,c_S + c_V + e\,c_L)$ per call, and saves nothing once $e \ge 1 - (a\,c_S + c_V)/c_L$. Second, specialization has fixed costs $F$: logging and curating traces, removing personal data, fine-tuning per call type, evaluation, router calibration, retraining under drift, and maintaining a fleet of specialists. Third, the large-model cost $c_L$ is not a list price: agent calls repeat long system prompts and tool schemas, which is the case that provider-side prompt caching and asynchronous batch pricing discount, and hosted fine-tuning of a provider's small model gives specialization without a fleet. Each of these lowers $c_L$ or $F$ for the comparator and so raises $N^{*}$, as does any further fall in hosted prices at fixed capability. Fourth, the comparator for server-side calls is not only a frontier model: a hosted general-purpose small model or a small-active-parameter \gls{moe} has small-model per-token cost and no specialization cost. The one measurement of cost per correct answer across model classes finds that lightweight models are cost-optimal on basic quantitative tasks and large models on knowledge-intensive ones \citep{erol2025costofpass} (\evB), which supports a conditional premise and not a general one. Finally, ``zero marginal cost'' on a device means no per-query charge to the operator, not zero cost to a user who pays in battery and thermal budget; offline, the large model cannot run at all, which makes the question one of capability rather than cost.

\subsubsection{P3: Control}
\label{subsubsec:agentic_alignment}

An agent needs its model to emit exactly the format the scaffold expects. Constrained decoding enforces well-formedness outside the model \citep{willard2023guided,dong2024xgrammar}, so syntactic validity for a supported schema does not depend on model size. This removes an objection to small models rather than giving them an advantage, since a large model can be constrained equally. What remains for P3 is the argument of \citet{belcak2025slm_agentic} that a narrow output distribution is easier to impose on a small model by fine-tuning on the agent's own traces than to elicit from a frontier model by prompting, together with operational properties: small models are easier to version, roll back, host on-premises, and co-locate with their tools.

\subsection{Agent-Specific Evidence}
\label{subsec:agentic_evidence}

General benchmark scores are the wrong instrument for these premises (Section~\ref{subsec:reported_agentic_gap}). This subsection assembles the evidence that bears on them, by the capability an agent exercises, and grades each line (Table~\ref{tab:agentic_evidence}).

\subsubsection{Function Calling and Tool Selection}
\label{subsubsec:agentic_ev_fc}

This is the best-evidenced capability. The Berkeley Function-Calling Leaderboard \citep{patil2025bfcl} checks function selection and argument filling with executable checks, in single-turn, parallel, and, from its third version, multi-turn settings; its predecessors \citep{patil2023gorilla,qin2024toolllm,li2023apibank} established that API-call generation is a skill on which general capability transfers only partially. Developer reports place specialized sub-10B models among the leading single-turn entries of 2024: APIGen's 7B model was reported to outperform several GPT-4 models and its 1B model GPT-3.5-Turbo \citep{liu2024apigen}; the xLAM-7B card places it third at 88.24\% (the first place belonged to an 8$\times$22B member of the series) \citep{zhang2024xlam}; ToolACE reports an 8B model rivalling GPT-4 \citep{liu2025toolace}; and on-device work reports 0.1--7B models competitive on narrower suites \citep{chen2024octopusv2,erdogan2024tinyagent,lin2024hammer,liu2024mobilellm}. Meta reports Llama~3.2 3B at 67.0 on BFCL~v2 against 67.1 for Llama~3.1 8B, and the 1B model at 25.7 \citep{meta2024llama32card}. For multi-turn use, APIGen-MT trains 1B--70B models on verified multi-turn trajectories and reports that they outperform GPT-4o and Claude~3.5 on $\tau$-bench and BFCL, with smaller models surpassing larger ones in multi-turn settings and more consistent across repeated trials \citep{prabhakar2025apigenmt}. All of these are developer reports on public benchmarks, and the placements were not checked against archived leaderboard snapshots (\evC).

Independent evidence qualifies them. \citet{kavathekar2025smallmodels} find that small models improve from zero- to few-shot to fine-tuned function calling but ``struggle significantly with adhering to the given output format'' (\evB), and \citet{shen2024weaktool} recover performance only by splitting planning, calling, and summarization across separately fine-tuned small models. Read together, the evidence supports the conditional form of H: per-call competence at this size is something specialization produces, not something an off-the-shelf small model has.

\subsubsection{Structured Output: Validity Is Not Correctness}
\label{subsubsec:agentic_ev_struct}

Constrained decoding guarantees that output parses, at an overhead its authors report as small \citep{willard2023guided,dong2024xgrammar} (\evC). Three results limit what that buys. JSONSchemaBench finds that frameworks differ in schema coverage, efficiency, and output quality, so the guarantee holds for the features a framework supports \citep{geng2025jsonschemabench} (\evB). Grammar-constrained decoding distorts the model's distribution, producing grammatical outputs whose likelihoods are not those the model assigns \citep{park2024gad}. And \citet{tam2024speakfreely} report that format restrictions degrade reasoning (\evB), a result disputed by the developers of a constrained-decoding library on methodological grounds \citep{kurt2024saywhatyoumean}, so its size is uncertain. What constrained decoding does not do is make the content correct, and the gap between well-formed and correct output, a valid call to the wrong tool or with a wrong argument value, is where small models still lose ground. This is why H4 requires a correctness check (H4b) and not only a validity check (H4a).

\subsubsection{End-to-End Agent Success}
\label{subsubsec:agentic_ev_e2e}

Here the evidence is weakest and partly contradictory. When the realistic multi-step benchmarks were introduced, success was low for every model: the best GPT-4 agent reached 14.41\% on WebArena against 78.24\% for humans \citep{zhou2024webarena}, GPT-4 with plugins 15\% on GAIA against 92\% \citep{mialon2024gaia}, and the best model about 2\% on SWE-bench \citep{jimenez2024swebench} (\evB). The one cross-class comparison of that period, AgentBench, found a large gap between commercial models and open models of 70B or less, attributed to long-horizon reasoning, decision-making, and instruction following \citep{liu2024agentbench} (\evB); it compared open with closed models of 2023, so model size is confounded with openness and training. Against it, the APIGen-MT developers report specialized small models ahead of frontier models on $\tau$-bench \citep{prabhakar2025apigenmt} (\evC). We found no dated, independent, like-for-like comparison of current specialized sub-10B agents and frontier agents, so whether a size gap persists for specialized small models is open (\evD).

The failure mode these benchmarks expose is the one per-call metrics hide. $\tau$-bench reports that even strong models succeed on under half of its tasks and degrade as the number of repeated trials grows \citep{yao2024taubench}, and $\tau^2$-bench reports further drops when the agent must coordinate with an active user \citep{barres2025tau2}. A trajectory of $n$ calls at per-call reliability $p$ completes at $p^n$ absent recovery, so the difference between 0.98 and 0.95 per call is the difference between 82\% and 60\% over ten steps. A correctness check that triggers escalation (H4b) is what breaks this compounding; a validity check alone lets valid but wrong outputs pass and compound.

\subsubsection{Routing, Escalation, and Cost per Completed Task}
\label{subsubsec:agentic_ev_routing}

Cascades and routers report large savings on single-turn query benchmarks: FrugalGPT matches the best single model with up to 98\% cost reduction \citep{chen2023frugalgpt}, Hybrid~LLM sends up to 40\% fewer calls to the large model with no quality drop \citep{ding2024hybridllm}, AutoMix reports over 50\% cost reduction at comparable quality \citep{aggarwal2024automix}, and RouteLLM over $2\times$ at matched quality with transfer to unseen model pairs \citep{ong2024routellm} (all \evC). RouterBench provides a common dataset of 405K inference outcomes for comparing routers \citep{hu2024routerbench}. None of these reports, inside an agent loop, how often the router is confidently wrong, how escalation rates move under distribution shift, or what the seam costs when a trajectory switches models.

Cost per correct outcome has begun to be measured, but not for the comparison H requires. Cost-of-pass, the expected monetary cost of a correct solution, has been measured across model classes on single-turn tasks \citep{erol2025costofpass} (\evB); the Holistic Agent Leaderboard reports agent accuracy and cost across 9 frontier-class models and 9 benchmarks, and finds, for example, that higher reasoning effort reduced accuracy in most runs \citep{kapoor2025hal} (\evB); and \citet{kapoor2024agentsmatter} argue that agents should be evaluated on joint cost and accuracy. We found no study that reports cost per completed task for an \gls{slm}-first agent against an all-\gls{llm} agent on the same workload. That is the measurement on which H is confirmed or refuted (Section~\ref{subsec:challenges_verification}).

\subsubsection{What This Evidence Supports}
\label{subsubsec:agentic_ev_summary}

The evidence supports a narrower claim than the one usually made. Developers report (\evC), and independent work qualifies (\evB), that a \emph{specialized} sub-10B model can perform the individual delegated operation at a standard competitive with much larger models; an off-the-shelf small model often cannot. Validity can be guaranteed independently of model size for supported schemas; correctness cannot. Whether a specialized small-model agent completes realistic multi-step tasks at a comparable rate is contested, and whether it does so at lower cost per completed task has not been measured. The case for H is therefore strongest where a correctness check exists and the call type recurs often enough to repay specialization, and unproven elsewhere.

\subsection{Where the Hypothesis Does Not Hold}
\label{subsec:agentic_counterexamples}

For the following workload classes the conditions of H fail and a frontier model remains the right default.

\begin{description}
  \item[Open-ended planning] When the agent must decide \emph{what} to do, the call fails H1: there is no fixed schema and the space of acceptable plans is large. AgentBench locates the open-model gap in long-horizon reasoning and decision-making \citep{liu2024agentbench}, and a planning error propagates to every later step.
  \item[Synthesis over long or heterogeneous context] H3 fails for large document sets, long trajectory histories, and multi-source retrieval requiring cross-document reasoning. Long context in a small model is a memory and prefill problem before it is a capability problem, and we found no evidence that small models close the gap on long-context synthesis (\evD).
  \item[Uncheckable output] H4b fails for open-ended generation, judgment calls, and tasks where verification is as hard as the task; errors then compound silently.
  \item[Low-resource-language and safety-sensitive calls] The multilingual and safety deficits of Section~\ref{subsec:reported_safety} are concentrated in small models, and a router that sends such calls to a small model on size grounds degrades them silently.
  \item[Sparse or unstable call distributions] H2 fails when a call type is rare, when traffic drifts faster than specialization can track (raising $F$), or when cached, batched, or fine-tuned hosted alternatives make $c_L$ low; a hosted model is then cheaper in total.
  \item[Cold start] Before deployment there are no logged traces for the conversion pipeline. The opening move is a frontier model with instrumentation, and migration afterwards.
\end{description}

Planning and uncheckable generation are not transitional: they are categories of work for which narrow competence and constrained output are the wrong properties, and the heterogeneous design below exists because of them.

\subsection{Heterogeneous SLM--LLM Systems}
\label{subsec:agentic_hetero}

For call types meeting H1--H4 the default inverts: \glspl{slm} handle them, and a generalist model handles planning, open-ended decomposition, long context, broad world knowledge, and calls whose small-model output fails a check \citep{belcak2025slm_agentic,sharma2025slm_agentic}. \subsubsection{Routing Designs}
\label{subsubsec:agentic_routing_summary}
Three designs recur: cascades that try the small model first and escalate on a quality signal \citep{chen2023frugalgpt,aggarwal2024automix}, predictive routers that decide before generation \citep{ding2024hybridllm,ong2024routellm}, and decomposing routers that assign sub-questions to a local or a cloud model \citep{division_of_thoughts_2025}. H prescribes a cascade whose quality signal is the H4 check. Speculative decoding \citep{leviathan2023speculative,chen2023specsampling,cai2024medusa,li2024eagle} speeds up the large model without delegating answers, and an escalated call can itself be served speculatively. \suppsec~\ref{subsubsec:agentic_routing} describes each.

\subsubsection{Production Instances}
\label{subsubsec:agentic_production}

Apple pairs an on-device model with a server model on Private Cloud Compute and routes to the server when the on-device model is expected to fall short \citep{gunter2025afm}; its 2026 models were built in collaboration with Google \citep{apple2026afm3,google_apple2026statement}. The design is motivated by privacy and latency as much as by cost, and Apple reports no cost per completed task, so it shows that the heterogeneous pattern is deployable at scale, not that it is cheaper. Google's Gemini Nano occupies the same on-device role on Android \citep{gemini2023}.

\subsection{Conversion and Adoption}
\label{subsec:agentic_conversion_summary}
For an agent that already runs on a frontier model, \citet{belcak2025slm_agentic} propose a six-step conversion: log the agent's calls, strip personal data, cluster them into recurring types, choose a candidate \gls{slm} per cluster, specialize it with \gls{lora} or \gls{qlora}, and repeat as traffic drifts. The clustering step is where H1, H3, and H4 can be checked and where the pilot estimates for H2 are made; clusters that fail stay with the generalist, and each converted cluster should be evaluated on cost per completed task against the unconverted agent. They also identify three barriers to adoption: sunk investment in centralized inference, evaluation on generalist leaderboards, and lower attention to \gls{slm} releases; Section~\ref{subsec:agentic_evidence} shows the second to be partly a real evidential gap. \suppsec~\ref{supp:agentic} gives the pipeline and barriers in full.
