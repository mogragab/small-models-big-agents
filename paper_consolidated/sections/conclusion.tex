\section{Conclusion}
\label{section:conclusion}

This paper set out to do two things at once: survey the small-language-model
landscape as it stands in 2026, and argue a position about where it is going. On
the first, we fixed a two-condition definition that separates the per-token cost
of a model from its residency cost, cataloged 215 documented \gls{slm} variants
under a multi-label faceted taxonomy, tracked the 2025--2026 shift toward hybrid
state-space architectures, unified reasoning models, and mobile-first multimodal
families, reviewed the compression techniques specific to the sub-10B regime,
where quantization outperforms pruning \citep{kurz2025pruning_vs_quant}, and
audited the public evaluation record for an 18-model cohort. That audit returned
a negative result: the record does not currently support the cross-model accuracy
comparison that surveys in this area routinely present.

On the second: the ``less is more'' thesis is no longer only about efficiency,
and it is not a universal claim. The consumer of language intelligence that is
growing fastest is the AI agent, and for a large share of what an agent
delegates, a generalist is the wrong instrument. Where a call is bounded,
recurrent, short-context, and cheaply verifiable, a small specialized model is
sufficiently capable, substantially cheaper to serve, cheaper to adapt, and
easier to hold to a code interface \citep{belcak2025slm_agentic}. Where it is
not, and the counterexamples of
Section~\ref{subsec:agentic_counterexamples} are neither few nor transitional,
a frontier model remains the right choice. The evidence assembled here supports
the premises unevenly, and we have graded them rather than averaging over them:
independent leaderboards support per-call competence at this size, and no
published study measures cost per completed task for a small-model agent against
a large-model one, which is the measurement the economic premise turns on. Apple's
on-device and server split \citep{gunter2025afm}, together with the routing
systems surveyed in Section~\ref{sec:agentic}, embodies the heterogeneous design
at production scale.
The barriers that remain are those of sunk infrastructure, measurement, and
attention, and the measurement barrier is the one this paper finds to be a real
evidential gap rather than only a misdirected emphasis.

The work ahead is concrete. Below roughly 3B parameters, task-specific
adaptation stops being optional and becomes a precondition for reliable
delegated work, and inference-time compute only partly closes the remaining
reasoning gap;
hallucination is worse at small scale; compression can amplify bias; hardware and
model architecture are still designed separately; and the consolidated evidence
base needs controlled verification of the handful of claims that practitioners
most depend on (Section~\ref{sec:challenges}). None of these is a reason to wait,
and the six experiments specified in
Section~\ref{subsec:challenges_verification} are the shortest route from a survey
of what has been reported to a body of what has been established.
The future of applied \gls{nlp} is not one frontier model answering every query
through a cloud API, and neither is it small models everywhere. It is a fabric of
small, specialized, mostly on-device models doing the ordinary work, calling a
large model on the planning, the long-context synthesis, and the calls nobody can
check. In that architecture, less is not a limitation. It is the design.
