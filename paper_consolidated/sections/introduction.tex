\section{Introduction}
\label{section:introduction}

The landscape of \gls{nlp} has been reshaped by \glspl{llm}. Systems such as GPT-4 \citep{openai2023gpt4}, Claude \citep{anthropic2024claude}, Gemini \citep{gemini2023}, and the Llama series \citep{grattafiori2024llama3} demonstrate broad competence across reasoning, code generation, and multilingual tasks. That competence, however, comes at extraordinary cost. GPT-3's 175~billion parameters \citep{brown2020gpt3} inaugurated an era in which scale was treated as synonymous with capability, and scaling laws \citep{kaplan2020scaling,hoffmann2022chinchilla} appeared to promise predictable returns on ever-larger models. Frontier \glspl{llm} now require multi-thousand-GPU clusters to train, tens of gigabytes of accelerator memory to serve, and cloud infrastructure that introduces latency, recurring cost, and privacy exposure. Training GPT-3 is estimated to have consumed 1{,}287~MWh and emitted roughly 552~metric tons of CO$_2$ equivalent \citep{patterson2021carbon}; a single frontier-model query draws on the order of ten times the energy of a conventional web search \citep{devries2023energy}.

\subsection{The Rise of Small Language Models}
\label{subsec:intro_rise}

Running against this trajectory is a counter-trend: \glspl{slm} that achieve disproportionately strong performance for their size. Section~\ref{subsec:intro_definition} fixes the definition we use throughout; informally, an \gls{slm} is an autoregressive language model small enough to be served on a single consumer accelerator, laptop, or phone. Where earlier compact models such as DistilBERT \citep{sanh2019distilbert} and TinyBERT \citep{jiao2020tinybert} were task-specific encoders, the current generation are general-purpose, instruction-following systems. The Chinchilla scaling analysis \citep{hoffmann2022chinchilla} showed that most large models were badly undertrained and that a smaller model given proportionally more data could match them; the Phi family then demonstrated that ``textbook-quality'' synthetic data lets a 3.8B model rival models an order of magnitude larger \citep{abdin2024phi3}, Gemma applied distillation from larger teachers \citep{gemma2024}, and Qwen2.5 pushed sub-7B training to 18~trillion tokens \citep{qwen2024qwen25}. MIT Technology Review named \glspl{slm} one of its 10 Breakthrough Technologies of 2025 \citep{mit2025breakthrough}. The 2025--2026 release wave adds hybrid state-space architectures \citep{falconh1_2025}, controllable-reasoning models \citep{qwen3_2025}, and mobile-first multimodal families \citep{gemma4_2026,gunter2025afm}. Figure~\ref{fig:cumulative_releases} shows the resulting acceleration: the cumulative count of documented open \gls{slm} variants has grown from a handful in 2023 to more than one hundred by early 2026.

\subsection{Definitions and Terminology}
\label{subsec:intro_definition}

Survey work on this class of models is complicated by the fact that ``small'' is
used to mean at least four different things. We therefore fix the terminology
before using it, and hold to it for the rest of the paper.

\subsubsection{The Operative Definition}
\label{subsubsec:intro_def_operative}

A model is a \emph{small language model} for the purposes of this survey when it
satisfies both of the following:

\begin{description}
  \item[C1 (compute envelope)] It is an autoregressive decoder-only or hybrid
    attention/state-space model whose \emph{active} parameter count per generated
    token is at most $2 \times 10^{10}$ (roughly twenty billion).
  \item[C2 (residency envelope)] Its \emph{total} parameter count is also at most
    roughly twenty billion, so that its weights can be resident in the memory of
    a single consumer accelerator, laptop, or high-end phone at 4-bit precision.
\end{description}

C1 bounds per-token latency, FLOPs, and energy. C2 bounds storage, download size,
and resident memory. The two coincide for dense models, which is why a single
threshold has been sufficient in most prior work, but they come apart for
\gls{moe} models, and the distinction matters for deployment: a 119B-total model
that activates 6.5B parameters per token has the inference economics of a 6.5B
model and the storage and memory economics of a 119B one. Reporting only active
parameters, as several vendor announcements and prior surveys do, systematically
understates what it costs to put such a model on a device.

\subsubsection{Four Distinguished Terms}
\label{subsubsec:intro_def_terms}

\begin{description}
  \item[\gls{slm}] A model satisfying both C1 and C2. This is the class the
    survey is about, and the class to which every deployment, memory, and
    on-device claim in this paper applies.
  \item[Compact model] An \gls{slm} at or below roughly 3B total parameters. This
    is the band in which task-specific adaptation stops being optional
    (Section~\ref{section:conclusion}) and in which 4-bit weights fit in
    approximately 2~GB.
  \item[Edge model] A model, of any size, for which the developer documents a
    target of phone-, single-board-computer-, or \gls{npu}-class hardware, and
    ships an artifact for it. ``Edge'' is a claim about the intended deployment
    target, not about parameter count, and the two are correlated but not
    identical.
  \item[Small-active-parameter \gls{moe}] A model satisfying C1 but not C2: at
    most roughly twenty billion active parameters per token, but more in total.
    These have \gls{slm}-like per-token cost and \gls{llm}-like residency cost.
    They are retained in the inventory, because they are a live and growing part
    of the design space, but they are labelled separately and are excluded from
    every claim in this paper about storage footprint, download size, resident
    memory, or on-device feasibility.
\end{description}

Of the 126 variants in the inventory (Appendix~\ref{appendix:ecosystem}), 13
report a distinct active-parameter count and 6 of those exceed the C2 threshold
on total parameters; the latter 6 are the small-active-parameter \gls{moe} group.
The 18-model cohort whose public evaluation record is examined in
Section~\ref{sec:reported} is a strict subset of the models satisfying both C1
and C2, and is further restricted to below 10B total parameters with Phi-4 (14B)
retained as a single upper-bound reference, because that is the band in which
comparable public reporting actually exists.

\subsubsection{Model-Artifact Vocabulary}
\label{subsubsec:intro_def_artifacts}

Because the inventory counts artifacts rather than papers, we also fix the
following. A \emph{family} is a named line of models sharing an architecture and
a training recipe (Qwen2.5, Gemma~3). A \emph{variant} is one released
parameter size within a family together with one post-training status
(Qwen2.5-3B base and Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct are two variants). A \emph{checkpoint}
is a specific set of weights published under a version identifier; a variant may
be revised through several checkpoints, and the inventory records the variant,
not each checkpoint. A \emph{release} is a dated publication event covering one
or more variants. A \emph{pretrained} (or base) model is the artifact produced by
pretraining alone; an \emph{instruction-tuned} model has undergone supervised
instruction fine-tuning and usually a preference-optimization stage; a
\emph{reasoning-tuned} model has additionally been trained, by distillation of
long chain-of-thought traces, reinforcement learning, or both, to emit extended
intermediate reasoning before its answer. These three post-training statuses are
not interchangeable for evaluation purposes, and conflating them is one of the
specific defects in the public record documented in
Section~\ref{subsec:reported_overall}.

Two conventions follow from this vocabulary and are used consistently throughout.
Family names are written as their developers write them and with a space before
the version, as in Gemma~3, Qwen2.5, Phi-4, and Llama~3.2; the original 2023
LLaMA release keeps its own capitalization, since Meta renamed the line to
``Llama'' from Llama~2 onward. Checkpoint identifiers are written in the
hyphenated form used on the model hub, as in \texttt{Gemma-3-4B-it} or
\texttt{Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct}, and are used wherever the specific artifact rather
than the family is meant.

\subsection{The Agentic Turn}
\label{subsec:intro_agentic}

At the same time, the way language models are consumed has shifted. The fastest-growing workload is not the chat session but the \emph{AI agent}: a control loop in which a model is called repeatedly to choose a tool, fill its arguments, extract a field, classify an intent, or check a result \citep{belcak2025slm_agentic,chen2024mas_survey}. These calls are narrow, repetitive, and format-constrained, and they exercise only a thin slice of a frontier model's competence. \citet{belcak2025slm_agentic} argue that for such workloads \glspl{slm} are not a compromise but the appropriate default: sufficiently capable for the delegated sub-task, cheaper to serve, cheaper to specialize, and easier to constrain to a code interface, with a strong generalist model reserved for the minority of calls that genuinely need planning or broad context. Surveys of LLM--SLM collaboration \citep{chen2024role_small,chen2025collab_survey} and production systems such as Apple's on-device/server split \citep{gunter2025afm} indicate that this heterogeneous design is already the direction of travel.

\subsection{The Thesis, Stated Conditionally}
\label{subsec:intro_thesis}

This paper is a survey with a position attached, and we state the position as a
conditional design hypothesis rather than a universal claim, because that is what
the available evidence supports.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{H (substrate hypothesis).} For a language-model call inside an agent that
is (i)~\emph{bounded}, in that the required output is a tool selection, a
structured record, a label from a fixed set, or a short local edit; (ii)~\emph{recurrent},
in that calls of the same type arrive often enough to repay one-time
specialization; (iii)~\emph{short-context}, in that the input needed to answer it
fits well inside the model's window without retrieval over a large corpus; and
(iv)~\emph{verifiable}, in that the surrounding scaffold can cheaply check whether
the output is well-formed and, ideally, correct, a specialized \gls{slm} is the
appropriate default execution substrate, and a frontier \gls{llm} should be
invoked only on escalation.
\end{quote}

The hypothesis is falsifiable and it is bounded. It does not claim that
\glspl{slm} match frontier models in general, that scaling has stopped paying, or
that agentic systems can dispense with large models. Section~\ref{subsec:agentic_counterexamples}
sets out the workload classes for which the antecedent fails and a large model
remains the right choice: open-ended planning over an unbounded action space,
synthesis over long or heterogeneous context, tasks whose correctness cannot be
checked without a stronger model, low-resource-language and safety-sensitive
calls where small models have documented deficits, and any workload whose call
distribution is too sparse or too unstable to specialize against.
Section~\ref{subsec:agentic_evidence} grades the evidence for each premise of H,
and is explicit that the evidence is strongest for premise (i) and weakest for
the end-to-end claim.

\subsection{Scope and Contributions}
\label{subsec:intro_gap}

Existing \gls{slm} surveys \citep{lu2024slmsurvey,vannguyen2024slm_survey,wang2024comprehensive,srivastava2025thinkslm} provide taxonomies and measurements but either predate the 2025--2026 model wave or treat agentic deployment only in passing; agentic position pieces \citep{belcak2025slm_agentic} make the argument but do not ground it in a consolidated capability-and-deployment review. This paper joins the two. Its contributions are:

\begin{description}
  \item[Landscape and taxonomy] A catalog of 126 documented \gls{slm} variants (2022--2026), including the recent hybrid, reasoning, and mobile-multimodal families, organized under a multi-label faceted taxonomy (Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy} and Appendix~\ref{appendix:ecosystem}), with a machine-readable release (Section~\ref{subsec:intro_artifacts}).
  \item[Compression review] A synthesis of distillation, quantization, pruning, and compound pipelines for the sub-10B regime, with the reduction achieved reported separately along parameter count, memory, training compute, and latency rather than collapsed into a single ratio, including the finding that quantization consistently outperforms pruning below 4B parameters \citep{kurz2025pruning_vs_quant} (Section~\ref{sec:compression}).
  \item[An audit of the public evaluation record] An examination of what the public reporting record can actually support for an 18-model cohort, and the finding that a cross-model accuracy comparison cannot be assembled from it without misrepresenting the sources, because vendors report non-intersecting benchmark sets, differing shot counts per benchmark, inconsistently labelled base and instruction-tuned checkpoints, and few matched baselines (Section~\ref{sec:reported}, Appendix~\ref{app:provenance}).
  \item[Deployment ecosystem] A survey of inference frameworks, quantization formats, and on-device platforms from Raspberry~Pi to Apple silicon, with calculated and measured quantities kept apart (Section~\ref{sec:deployment}).
  \item[The agentic argument, with agent-specific evidence] A structured, evidence-graded case for hypothesis~H, drawing on function-calling, tool-use, structured-output, and end-to-end agent benchmarks rather than general model scores, together with its stated counterexamples (Section~\ref{sec:agentic}).
  \item[Open problems and verification] An agenda of open research problems and targeted empirical-verification opportunities for the consolidated evidence base (Section~\ref{sec:challenges}).
\end{description}

\subsection{Survey Method and Sources}
\label{subsec:intro_method}

This is a structured survey conducted under a documented protocol. It is not a
PRISMA systematic review: the aim is representative coverage of the \gls{slm}
design space and a complete audit of the public performance record for a defined
cohort, not an exhaustive enumeration of every publication in the area. What
follows is the protocol in full, so that the search can be repeated and its
coverage assessed.

\subsubsection{Research Questions}
\label{subsubsec:intro_rqs}

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1] What is the design space of small language models released between 2022 and 2026, and along which facets do the released artifacts actually differ?
  \item[RQ2] Which compression techniques are effective in the sub-10B regime, and along which axis (parameters, memory, training compute, latency, quality) does each one pay?
  \item[RQ3] What does the public evaluation record support about the relative capability of these models, and what does it not support?
  \item[RQ4] What does the evidence say about the suitability of small models for agentic workloads specifically, as opposed to general benchmarks?
  \item[RQ5] Under what conditions is a small model the appropriate default inside an agent, and under what conditions is it not?
\end{description}

\subsubsection{Search Protocol}
\label{subsubsec:intro_search}

Three bodies of evidence were assembled, each under an explicit rule.

\emph{Model documentation (RQ1).} Candidate variants were identified from four
starting points: (a)~the Hugging Face Hub model index, filtered to
text-generation models with a published model card, sorted by downloads and by
recency, and queried for the family names of every developer already known to
ship in this band; (b)~vendor release channels for Microsoft, Google DeepMind,
Meta, Alibaba, Mistral AI, IBM, NVIDIA, TII, Apple, Hugging Face, AI2, Stability
AI, DeepSeek, Liquid AI, and Cohere; (c)~arXiv listings under cs.CL and cs.LG for
technical reports announcing a model release; and (d)~forward citation tracing
from the four prior \gls{slm} surveys named below. Searches were run over the
collection window \needsdata{search window: first and last search dates} and
cover releases dated up to \needsdata{last release date included}.

\emph{Technique literature (RQ2, RQ3).} The ACL Anthology, arXiv (cs.CL, cs.LG),
and the proceedings of NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, EMNLP, ACL, and MLSys for 2019--2026
were searched with the Boolean query

\begin{quote}\ttfamily\footnotesize
(``small language model'' OR ``compact language model'' OR ``efficient language
model'' OR ``on-device language model'' OR ``edge LLM'') AND (survey OR
quantization OR pruning OR distillation OR ``parameter-efficient'' OR inference
OR deployment OR agent OR ``tool use'' OR routing)
\end{quote}

\noindent restricted to title, abstract, and keywords, seeded from
\citet{lu2024slmsurvey}, \citet{vannguyen2024slm_survey},
\citet{wang2024comprehensive}, and \citet{belcak2025slm_agentic}, and expanded by
one round of forward and backward citation tracing from every retained work.

\emph{The evaluation record (RQ3).} For each model in the 18-model cohort, the
primary release document, the official model card, and any linked evaluation
appendix were retrieved in full, together with community standardized evaluations
and third-party reproductions where they existed. The collection and appraisal
rules are set out in Section~\ref{subsec:reported_protocol}. No benchmark was
executed for this survey.

\subsubsection{Screening, Retrieval Counts, and Agreement}
\label{subsubsec:intro_screening}

Deduplication was performed on normalized title plus first author, and then on
arXiv identifier and DOI; the duplicate-identifier check is what surfaced the one
bibliographic collision that survived into an earlier draft of this paper.
Records were screened on title and abstract against the inclusion criteria, and
retained records were read in full. Table~\ref{tab:screening} gives the flow of
records through those stages; the per-record decisions are in the released
screening ledger (Section~\ref{subsec:intro_artifacts}).

Inclusion criteria for the technique literature were: (T1)~the work concerns
models at or below roughly twenty billion parameters, or a compression,
adaptation, routing, or deployment technique whose reported evaluation includes
that regime; (T2)~it is available as a peer-reviewed paper, an archived preprint,
or a vendor technical report with a stable identifier; and (T3)~it reports enough
methodological detail for a reader to determine what was measured. Exclusion
reasons were recorded per record and are summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:screening}.

Inclusion criteria for model variants were: (M1)~a citable primary source
describing architecture and training; (M2)~a public artifact or a documented
evaluation card; (M3)~satisfaction of C1, with C2 status recorded separately; and
(M4)~a dated release. Community fine-tunes, quantized re-uploads, and merges of
other people's checkpoints were excluded, as were variants that differ from a
listed variant only in serialization format.

Screening was carried out by \needsdata{number} of the authors working
independently, with disagreements resolved by discussion; the agreement statistic
at the title-and-abstract stage is \needsdata{percent agreement and Cohen's
$\kappa$}. Quality appraisal of retained empirical works used four criteria,
recorded per work in the released ledger: whether the evaluation harness is
named, whether the evaluation conditions (shots, prompt, decoding) are stated,
whether a matched baseline is reported, and whether the artifact evaluated is
identified to the checkpoint. These four criteria are also what
Section~\ref{sec:reported} audits the vendor record against, so the appraisal
scheme and the audit instrument are deliberately the same.

\subsubsection{Released Artifacts}
\label{subsec:intro_artifacts}

To make the inventory checkable rather than merely stated, the following are
released alongside the paper: the machine-readable variant inventory
(\file{merged\_llm\_database.json}) with one record per variant carrying every
field in Table~\ref{tab:reporting_dimensions} plus source URL, source retrieval
date, license, and inclusion decision; the screening ledger recording every
retrieved record and its inclusion or exclusion reason; the figure- and
table-generation scripts, so that every generated figure in this paper can be
reproduced from the inventory; and a changelog keyed to the snapshot dates. These
are to be deposited in a versioned archival repository under a persistent
identifier before submission, and that identifier is stated in the Data
Availability declaration (Section~\ref{section:declarations}). Because the
repository is versioned, a reader can retrieve the exact snapshot underlying this
paper as well as any later one, which is what makes the update policy of
Section~\ref{subsubsec:intro_snapshot} checkable rather than merely promised.

\subsubsection{Snapshot and Update Policy}
\label{subsubsec:intro_snapshot}

This paper reports the state of the record as of 4~September 2026, with model
releases through 31~August 2026. The field moves faster than a journal review
cycle, so we state in advance how the snapshot is maintained. Releases appearing
between submission and acceptance are added to the released inventory, with their
addition recorded in the changelog, and are discussed in the text only where they
change a conclusion rather than merely extending a list; the paper's tables and
figures are regenerated from the inventory at the last revision before
acceptance, and the snapshot date on each is updated accordingly. Works appearing
during review that bear on RQ2--RQ5 are added to the related-work synthesis in
Section~\ref{section:background}. We do not claim the inventory is permanently
current, and the released artifact is the authority on what it contained at any
given date.
