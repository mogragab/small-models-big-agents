  \begin{abstract}
  Large Language Models (LLMs) with hundreds of billions of parameters
  have advanced natural language processing dramatically, but their cost,
  memory demand, and latency make them a poor fit for the fastest-growing
  consumer of language intelligence: the AI agent, which issues many
  narrow, repetitive, well-specified model calls rather than a few
  open-ended ones. Small Language Models (SLMs) have closed much of the
  capability gap through knowledge distillation, synthetic-data curation,
  and post-training quantization. This paper is a combined survey and
  position statement, and it fixes a definition the literature currently
  leaves loose: an SLM is a decoder-only or hybrid transformer bounded at
  roughly twenty billion parameters on \emph{both} active parameters per
  token, which governs latency and energy, and total parameters, which
  governs storage and resident memory. Models meeting only the first bound
  are distinguished as small-active-parameter mixtures of experts and
  excluded from on-device claims. We catalog 126 publicly documented SLM
  variants (2022--2026), including the 2025--2026 wave of hybrid
  state-space, unified-reasoning, and mobile-first multimodal families,
  under a multi-label faceted taxonomy, and release the inventory in
  machine-readable form. We review compression for the sub-10B regime,
  reporting what each technique reduces along five separate axes rather
  than as one ratio, and finding that quantization outperforms pruning
  below 4B parameters. We then audit the public evaluation record for an
  18-model cohort and report a negative result: a cross-model accuracy
  table cannot be assembled from current public reporting without
  misrepresenting its sources, because vendors report non-intersecting
  benchmark sets, shot counts that differ per benchmark and per vendor,
  base and instruction-tuned figures that are routinely conflated, and few
  matched baselines. Against that record we state the agentic case as a
  conditional hypothesis rather than a universal claim: for calls that are
  bounded, recurrent, short-context, and cheaply verifiable, a specialized
  SLM is the appropriate default execution substrate, with a frontier model
  on escalation. We grade the evidence for each premise using
  function-calling, tool-use, structured-output, and end-to-end agent
  benchmarks rather than general model scores, finding it strong at the
  level of the individual call and absent at the level of cost per
  completed task, and we state the workload classes for which the
  hypothesis fails. We survey heterogeneous SLM--LLM routing and the
  LLM-to-SLM conversion pipeline, and close with the open problems and a
  specification of the six experiments that would supply the controlled
  evidence this field does not yet have.
  \end{abstract}

  \begin{highlights}
  \item Fixes a two-condition definition of a small language model that separates per-token cost (active parameters) from residency cost (total parameters), and distinguishes small-active-parameter mixtures of experts from SLMs proper.
  \item Surveys 126 documented small language model variants (2022--2026), including the 2025--2026 hybrid state-space, unified-reasoning, and mobile-multimodal waves, under a multi-label faceted taxonomy, released in machine-readable form.
  \item Audits the public evaluation record for 18 SLMs and shows that it does not support the cross-model accuracy comparisons routinely published in surveys, leaderboards, and secondary sources.
  \item Reviews compression for the sub-10B regime, separating parameter, memory, training-compute, latency, and quality effects rather than collapsing them into one ratio, and finding that quantization outperforms pruning below 4B parameters.
  \item States the agentic case as a conditional hypothesis, grades the evidence for each premise against agent-specific benchmarks, and sets out the workload classes for which the hypothesis fails.
  \item Specifies six experiments that would supply the controlled evidence the field currently lacks, including the cost-per-completed-task comparison the economic argument turns on.
  \end{highlights}
