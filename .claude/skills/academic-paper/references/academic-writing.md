# Academic writing craft

The difference between a paper that reads as scholarship and one that reads as generated
text is almost entirely at the paragraph level.

## Synthesis, not annotation

The core move. A body paragraph advances a claim of *yours*, supported by several
sources; it does not walk through sources one at a time.

**Annotation (avoid):**
> Smith (2021) studied grid storage in Germany and found costs fell. Jones (2022)
> examined the UK market and reported similar results. Chen (2023) analysed Japan and
> concluded that costs also declined.

**Synthesis (aim for):**
> The cost decline appears robust across market structures: it holds in Germany's
> feed-in-tariff regime (Smith, 2021), the UK's capacity market (Jones, 2022), and
> Japan's vertically integrated utilities (Chen, 2023). What differs is the mechanism —
> Smith attributes it to procurement scale, whereas Chen finds the effect concentrated
> in cell chemistry improvements, a divergence that matters for whether the trend
> continues.

The second version has the same three citations and does three things the first does
not: it states a claim, it uses the sources as evidence for that claim, and it surfaces
a disagreement worth discussing later.

## Paragraph shape

Most body paragraphs run 100–180 words and follow: **claim → evidence → interpretation →
link**. The first sentence should be readable on its own as a proposition. If a
paragraph's first sentence is "Several studies have examined this topic," the paragraph
has no claim yet.

One idea per paragraph. If you need "Additionally" twice, you have two paragraphs.

## Signposting

Readers navigate long documents by skimming. Give them:
- A roadmap sentence at the end of the Introduction (in longer papers).
- A one- or two-sentence opener under each numbered section before the first
  subsection heading — never stack `2.` directly on top of `2.1` with nothing between.
- Transitions that state the logical relation ("These results hold only under
  steady-state assumptions; the following section relaxes that constraint"), not filler
  ("Moving on to the next topic").

## Hedging and strength calibration

Match the strength of the verb to the strength of the evidence. This is not timidity;
overclaiming is the fastest way for a reviewer to lose trust in a paper.

| Evidence | Language |
|---|---|
| Meta-analysis, replicated | demonstrates, establishes |
| Multiple consistent studies | indicates, shows |
| Single study, decent design | suggests, reports |
| Correlational, small n, preliminary | is associated with, may indicate |
| Theoretical or your inference | appears to, arguably, we propose |

Never write "proves" outside mathematics. Never assert causation from correlational
work; write "associated with" and mean it.

## Register

- Third person and impersonal by default; `we` is standard in STEM and increasingly
  accepted elsewhere. `I` is fine in single-author humanities work. Avoid `you`.
- Past tense for what was done and found ("participants completed"); present tense for
  established knowledge and for what the paper does ("this section argues").
- Precise over emphatic. Cut `very`, `extremely`, `hugely`, `truly`, `incredibly`.
- Formal but not inflated. `use` beats `utilise`; `about` beats `with regard to`.
- Define technical terms at first use; then use them consistently. Do not elegantly vary
  terminology — in a paper, a different word implies a different concept.
- No contractions, no rhetorical questions as section content, no exclamation marks.

## Prose that reads as machine-generated

These patterns are recognisable and undermine credibility regardless of content:

- Opening with "In today's rapidly evolving landscape" or any variant of "In an
  increasingly X world."
- `delve`, `tapestry`, `realm`, `testament to`, `navigate the complexities`,
  `paradigm shift` (unless discussing Kuhn), `game-changer`, `unlock the potential`.
- `It is important to note that` / `It is worth mentioning that` — usually deletable
  with no loss.
- Tricolon everywhere: "efficient, scalable, and robust" three times per page.
- "Not only… but also" as a default connective.
- Bulleted lists inside body sections where prose is expected. Lists belong in methods
  steps, criteria, and contributions — not in a discussion.
- Every section ending with a summary sentence that restates the section.
- Uniform paragraph length throughout. Real writing varies; a 60-word paragraph after
  three 150-word ones creates emphasis.
- Hollow conclusions: "Further research is needed in this area." Say *what* research,
  *why* it is needed, and what it would resolve.

## Section-specific notes

**Abstract.** Written last. One or two sentences each on context, gap, method,
principal findings *with the actual numbers*, and implication. No citations, no
abbreviations undefined, no "this paper will."

**Introduction.** Funnel: field-level significance → the specific problem → what is
known → what is not known → what this paper does → contributions. The gap statement is
the pivot; make it explicit ("What remains unclear is whether…").

**Literature/background.** Thematic, critical, and ending with the gap you named in the
introduction. Evaluate, do not merely report: whose method was strongest, where the
field disagrees, what has not been tested.

**Methods.** Enough detail for replication. Past tense, procedural order, and honest
about deviations. For a review, this is the search strategy: databases, query strings,
date range, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and the number of records at each stage.

**Results.** Findings only, no interpretation. Report effect sizes and uncertainty, not
just significance. Do not restate every table cell in prose — point to the table and
narrate the pattern.

**Discussion.** Interpretation, in this order: what the findings mean, how they sit with
prior work (including what they contradict), why they might have come out this way,
what follows practically and theoretically, and what the limitations are. Write
limitations specifically and without apology — vague self-deprecation ("as with all
research, limitations exist") is worse than none.

**Conclusion.** Short. The answer to the research question, the contribution, and the
most important next step. No new citations, no new claims.

## Length control

If a section is over budget, the fix is almost never trimming words evenly; it is
cutting a paragraph that repeats another. If a section is under budget, the fix is
almost never padding; it is that the section is missing evidence or a
counter-consideration — go back to the ledger.
