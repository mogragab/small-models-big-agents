\begin{abstract}
  Large language models (LLMs) fit poorly, on cost, memory, and latency, with a
  growing class of workloads: AI agents that issue many narrow, repetitive,
  well-specified model calls. Small language models (SLMs) have narrowed the
  capability gap through distillation, curated and synthetic data, and
  quantization. This survey defines an SLM as an autoregressive decoder-only
  model of at most about twenty billion parameters both active per token, which
  bounds compute, and in total, which bounds storage; models meeting only the
  first bound are treated separately as small-active-parameter mixtures of
  experts. We catalog 179 SLMs and 30 such mixtures released from 2023 to
  September 2026, check every record's developer source, and release the
  inventory, the checks, and the scripts that apply the inclusion rule. We review
  compression along separate axes of parameters, memory, training compute, and
  latency. We quantify why the vendor evaluation record cannot support
  cross-model comparison: for the 14 cohort models of the four largest vendors,
  no benchmark is reported for all of them and no stated protocol is shared by
  more than five. We then state the agentic case as a conditional, falsifiable
  hypothesis: for call types that are bounded, recurrent enough to clear a
  break-even volume counting retries, checks, and escalations, short-context,
  and checkable for correctness, a specialized SLM should be the default, with
  escalation to a frontier model when a check fails. Grading the agent-specific
  evidence, we find per-call competence reported for specialized sub-10B models
  but qualified by independent studies, contested evidence on end-to-end
  success, and no measurement of cost per completed task for an SLM-first
  agent. We state the workloads for which the hypothesis fails and specify a
  pre-registered comparison, with its decision rule and sample size, that would
  refute it for a given workload.
\end{abstract}
