\section{Deployment on Resource-Constrained Environments}
\label{sec:deployment}

\subsection{On-Device Deployment and What It Guarantees}
\label{subsec:deployment_mobile}
\label{subsec:deployment_privacy_scope}

On-device inference removes the network round trip, the per-token charge, and the transmission of the prompt to an inference provider, and it works offline. It does not by itself keep user data on the device. Whether data leaves depends on whether the application emits telemetry or crash reports containing prompt content, logs conversations to a synchronized store, escalates calls to a cloud model (in which case, under difficulty-based routing, the escalated calls may be exactly the sensitive ones), or sends the same content to a hosted retrieval index or remote tool. On-device inference removes one egress channel, and eliminates egress only in an application whose whole pipeline is local and whose telemetry is absent or content-free. Apple's design \citep{gunter2025afm} treats the escalation path as the hard part and builds Private Cloud Compute around it. Every privacy claim in this paper is subject to this scope condition.

\subsubsection{Platforms and Device Tiers}
\label{subsubsec:deployment_platforms}

Apple's Foundation Models are the most fully documented on-device deployment: the 2025 generation pairs a roughly 3B on-device model, quantized with 2-bit quantization-aware training and \gls{kv}-cache sharing, with a server model on Private Cloud Compute \citep{gunter2025afm}, and the 2026 generation adds a 20B-total on-device model that activates 1--4B parameters and keeps its full weights in flash rather than DRAM \citep{apple2026afm3}. Apple describes the 2026 family as built in collaboration with Google \citep{apple2026afm3}, following a joint statement that its next foundation models would be based on Gemini models and cloud technology \citep{google_apple2026statement}. Gemini Nano plays the on-device role on Android \citep{gemini2023}. A current phone leaves roughly 4--8~GB to applications, so a 3B model at 4 bits (about 2~GB) fits, subject to thermal throttling and battery cost. Single-board computers and Jetson-class modules run quantized 1--7B models, and microcontrollers are out of reach for any model in the inventory (\suppsec~\ref{supp:deployment}). Table~\ref{tab:deployment_frameworks} (\suppsec~\ref{supp:tables}) compares the main runtimes: llama.cpp, ONNX Runtime, MLX, TensorRT-LLM, ExecuTorch, MLC-LLM, and WebLLM.

\subsection{Calculated Quantities Versus Measured Ones}
\label{subsec:deployment_latency}
\label{subsec:deployment_calc_vs_meas}

Weight memory at a precision, \gls{kv}-cache size per token, and their ratio follow from parameter count, bit-width, and head configuration, so they are reliable within their assumptions (Table~\ref{tab:memory_footprint}). Throughput, time-to-first-token, sustained rates under thermal load, energy per token, battery cost, and total cost of ownership are measured quantities that depend on batch size, context length, kernels, achieved bandwidth, runtime version, thermal envelope, and for energy the grid mix and the amortization of the device. Published figures for them are not comparable across sources, and we do not tabulate them; where one appears, it is attributed to its source and hardware.

One widely repeated claim needs particular care: that quantizing from 16 to 4 bits gives a near-linear $4\times$ speedup. It holds only for single-stream decoding at batch size~1 on memory-bandwidth-bound hardware, where every generated token reads the full weight set. Under batching the bottleneck moves toward compute, during the compute-bound prefill phase the gain largely disappears, on hardware without native low-precision kernels dequantization consumes part of it, and the memory saving is itself $2.8$--$3.6\times$ rather than $4\times$. Meta's measurements on a OnePlus~12 phone show the size of the shortfall: 4-bit Llama~3.2 1B and 3B decode $2.6\times$ faster than their BF16 versions, and their resident memory falls by 40--50\% \citep{meta2024llama32card} (\evC). The defensible statement is that 4-bit quantization converts a storage saving into a decode-time saving in proportion to the bandwidth it frees, and that single-stream interactive use, the dominant on-device regime, is where it does so.

Two further constraints bind on the edge. Prefill is compute-bound and sets time-to-first-token, which grows with context length; decode is bandwidth-bound and sets tokens per second. \Gls{gqa} and local--global attention shrink the \gls{kv} cache that every decode step reads, which is why they matter more on a device than on a server. For a bounded call that a small model completes correctly, these properties make the small model cheaper per call and faster once the network hop is counted; whether it is cheaper per completed task, once retries, checks, and escalations are counted, is the question Section~\ref{sec:agentic} takes up.
